The Bill of Rights in Ten Pages

Ten pages. One amendment each. Every one of them exists because something happened to somebody, and this book starts there instead of with the definition.

Four editions, kindergarten to high school. Made for one child at a kitchen table or twenty in a co-op.

The idea before the task

Ten things that went wrong

Every amendment in the Bill of Rights began as a complaint. Somebody was jailed for printing, taxed for a church they did not attend, searched without cause, or made to feed soldiers in their own kitchen. The Bill of Rights is the list of things that had already gone wrong. Here is the complaint behind each one, the way the booklet tells it to a third grader.

Amendment 1

Five Freedoms

A printer could be jailed for what she printed. A family paid taxes to a church they did not attend. People were arrested for meeting to complain. All of that is in one sentence now, turned around.

Amendment 2

Ready in a Minute

In 1775 there was no standing army to call. A town's defense was the town's own farmers, using what they already owned at home. They were called minutemen because that is how long they had to be ready.

Amendment 3

Whose Barn Is It?

Here is what most books get wrong. The law did not let soldiers move into your kitchen. It let the governor take your barn, your shed, and any empty building you owned, and you could not say no. You also had to help feed the soldiers.

Amendment 4

Ask First

Customs officers carried a paper called a writ of assistance. It let them search any house, any shop, any chest, for anything, on any day. It never ran out. It did not have to say what they were hunting for.

Amendment 5

Five Rules in One

Most people know one piece of this amendment from television. There are five. It is the longest one in the Bill of Rights because five different bad things all needed stopping at once.

Amendment 6

Not Far Away

Colonists could be put on a ship and tried in Britain, three thousand miles from anyone who saw what happened. No neighbor could speak for you. The Declaration of Independence listed that as a complaint.

Amendment 7

Who Decides?

This one is not about crime at all. It is about arguments over money and property. In the colonies, some courts decided those with one royal judge and no jury. So they wrote down that twelve ordinary neighbors get to decide instead.

Amendment 8

Is It Fair?

A punishment should match what was done, the way two sides of a scale should match. Too big is not allowed. What counts as too big has changed a lot since 1791.

Amendment 9

Not On The List

Some of the founders did not want a Bill of Rights at all. Their worry was simple. If you write down eight freedoms, somebody will later say those eight are the only ones you get. So they added a ninth that says the opposite.

Amendment 10

Near and Far

Thirteen states had just finished fighting a government far across an ocean. They were not eager to build another distant government with unlimited power. So the last amendment says it plainly. If we did not hand it over, we kept it.
